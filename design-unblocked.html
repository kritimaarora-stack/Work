Scaling Design Through AI

I used AI to enable 20 PMs to prototype on their own — so my 5 designers could focus on the hard problems.

Result8 → 3.5 days design-to-dev handoff~1 month → 12 days on large products
01The problem

The problem wasn't ambition. It was capacity.

A complex, operationally heavy product ecosystem. Five designers, around twenty PMs — and every request entered the same Design queue.

5Designers
~20Product Managers
The Design queue

Six kinds of request. One queue. Every one waited for a designer.

01A quick UI fix for Engineering
02A small interaction change
03An operational workflow experiment
04A prototype for a pilot
05A new product requirement
06A completely new flow needing stakeholder validation

Design had become the bottleneck.

Not because the team was slow — because every request, big or small, had to pass through the same five people.

Instead of asking

“How can my designers work faster?”

I asked

“Why does every one of these things need to pass through a designer?”

So I split the work by complexity
Tier ADesigner-led
High-impact design workStrategy, complex UX, new systems, critical journeys and final design decisions.
Tier BProduct + Design
Exploratory & experimentalPrototypes, operational experiments, early validation and pilots.
Tier CAI-assisted · Product-led
Small, repeatable workSmall changes, early prototypes and visual QA — work a designer didn't need to start from zero.

The goal wasn't to remove Design. It was to remove unnecessary dependency on it.

02Fixing the front door

Before touching AI, I fixed the workflow underneath it.

There was no real intake between Product and Design. Requirements mostly arrived with gaps — missing context, unclear goals, no shared idea of “done” — and Design was left to fill them in, in a loop:

PM idea→Designer asks questions→More context→First design→Stakeholder feedback→More clarification→Repeat

A lightweight Design Intake: the minimum information to decide whether something needs Design — and which tier.

  1. Q1What are we solving?
  2. Q2Why does it matter?
  3. Q3Who is affected?
  4. Q4What needs to be designed?
  5. Q5What needs to be tested?
  6. Q6What does “done” look like?

Pressure-tested with Product so it would actually be adopted.

03Five moves

Not one giant “AI transformation” — five small fixes, each aimed at one bottleneck.

01

Teaching Product to prototype with AI

Selected PMs learned agentic design workflows in Figma — inside a safe operating model: use the Design System, pair with a designer, get vetted before dev.

BeforePM idea→Design queue→Designer builds every step

AfterPM prototypes with AI→Designer vets→Ready for dev

02

Making AI speak the Design System

With the Design Systems team, I connected the AI workflow to our components and patterns — so 20 PMs wouldn't create 20 versions of our UI.

BeforeUnconstrained AI→A new UI language every time

AfterAI + Design System→Familiar components, ready to evolve

03

Figma became the collaboration layer

PMs moved from Lovable into Figma Make, so Product and Design commented on and iterated over the same artifact.

BeforeLovable prototype→Designer decodes intent→Recreates flow→Applies Design System→Preps for production

AfterPM idea→AI prototype in Figma Make→Design clarification→Ops validation→Production design

04

From legacy screens to usable design inputs

I connected legacy product APIs with Claude to extract existing flows — replacing manual archaeology with a structured starting point.

BeforeScreen recording→Screenshots→Reconstruct the flow→Translate to Design System→Start designing

AfterLegacy APIs→Claude extracts flows→Brought into Figma→Design starts on the new problem

05

Taking Visual QA out of the Design queue

AI flags the obvious — spacing, alignment, missing elements — before a designer looks. PMs later adopted it for their own products.

BeforeOpen build→Compare to design→Document mismatches→Send back→Repeat

AfterBuild→AI checks the obvious→Designer reviews

04Bringing people along

The hardest part wasn't the tooling. It was trust — on both sides.

Designers: “Why train PMs to do our job?”

Designers were hesitant to train PMs. I framed it as the way to free their bandwidth for high-impact work — and it changed their role: today they work as experts PMs consult, not as tools executing PM requests.

PMs: “Figma Make isn't as good as Lovable.”

PMs pushed back — they were new to Figma Make and biased toward Lovable. So I ran an experiment: the same prompt in both tools. The results were comparable, Figma Make's arguably better — and far easier for Design to translate into production.

05What changed

Handoffs got faster. Feedback came earlier.

Production-readiness turnaround · design → dev handoff

Measured across 4 roadmap projects over 4 months — 2 medium and 2 large — tracked myself, project by project.

What drove it: Product and Design prototyping together in Figma Make cut the clarification loop and the rebuild from PM prototype to design file. The review SLA then removed ~2 days of waiting on PM feedback per review round.

Product became self-sufficient

PMs explored and prototyped smaller changes before bringing work to Design.

Design got breathing room

More time for complex UX, systems thinking and design quality.

Feedback moved earlier

Operations reacted to real prototypes, not abstract requirements.

Prototypes weren't thrown away

Exploration happened in Figma, so approved ideas became the production starting point.

06The lesson & my role

AI didn't replace Design. It redistributed it across the Product organisation.

PMs handleExploration
AI handlesRepetition
Designers focus onComplexity

My job as Design Manager: designing the system that connects all three.

  1. An experiment in my pod“How can this team move faster?”

  3. Now: scaling the modelRolling out across Product pods.

What I did

Diagnosed the bottleneck→Redesigned the intake→Tested tools→Trained PMs & designers→Partnered with Design Systems→Wrote the Design × Product SOP→Scaled beyond my pod

The 30-second version

I removed Design as the bottleneck for 20 PMs by giving them AI prototyping inside our design system — so 5 designers could focus on the hard problems.

The problem
5 designers, ~20 PMs, one queue. Small fixes and big flows all waited for a designer, while complex work stalled.
What I did
Fixed intake, split work into three tiers by complexity, and taught PMs to prototype in Figma Make using our Design System. Claude turned legacy screens into structured flows; AI took the first pass at visual QA.
The result
Across 4 roadmap projects over 4 months, design-to-dev handoff dropped from 8 to 3.5 working days on medium items and from ~1 month to 12 days on large ones. A new SOP cut PM review time from ~3 days to 1. The model is now scaling across Product pods.
